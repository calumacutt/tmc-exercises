## Purpose

The purpose of this playbook is to give TMC coaches a repeatable process for writing a 6-week class program.

Right now that process lives in one person’s head. This playbook turns it into steps anyone can follow.

The goal is to:

- Cover the full breadth of movement over time, not just what we like teaching
- Make sure the important things come around often enough to actually progress
- Give members a visible sense of progress across a block
- Keep classes varied, balanced and fun
- Produce a program another coach can pick up and teach without asking questions
- Make the reasoning visible, so it can be reviewed and improved

## North Star Principle

### Program for the gaps, not for your favourites.

Left to instinct, every coach programs what they enjoy teaching and what they are already good at. Over a few blocks that quietly narrows what the members get.

The job is to look at what has NOT been trained recently, and deliberately bring it back.

Everything in this playbook is built around that one idea: look back before you look forward.

## What Success Looks Like

- Every discipline is trained at roughly its intended frequency
- Keystone exercises come around often enough for members to actually progress at them
- No block looks like a repeat of the one before it
- Every class has a warm up and a sensible spread of intensity
- Any coach can read the program and teach it without asking what was meant
- The program is written up in the standard format before the block starts
- Teachers have reviewed it before it runs
- It gets reviewed two weeks in, not just at the end

And members leave a block feeling:

- They got better at something specific
- They were exposed to something they had never tried
- The classes were varied, not repetitive

# 🔹 GLOSSARY

Five words with specific meanings at TMC. Get these straight before you program.

### Exercise

A single movement. “Bear Walk”, “Front Lever - Tuck”, “3 Ball Cascade”.

The smallest unit we program. Names are exact and unique — two exercises never share a name, because the name is how everything else refers to them.

### Line

A progression thread inside a discipline. “Vertical Pull”, “Front Split Line”, “Juggling”.

A line is the path from easy to hard for one specific capacity. If you want someone to get better at something, you move them along a line.

### Discipline

A coherent area of practice, made up of several lines. “Hanging & Pulling”, “Hand Balancing & Inversion”, “Object Play”. There are 15.

This is the level at which we think about COVERAGE. “We have not done any Object Play for three blocks” is a discipline-level observation, and it is the kind that matters most.

### Pillar

The five broad families the disciplines group into: Flocomotion, Strength & Capacity, Handstands & Balance, Object Play, Mobility.

Mostly useful for stepping back at the end and sanity-checking overall balance.

### Keystone

A landmark exercise that a lot of other training feeds into. “Handstand - Freestanding”, “Muscle Up - Rings”, “Front Splits”, “Push Up”. There are 27.

A keystone is not simply a hard exercise. It is one that:

- members recognise, want, and can name as a goal
- is built out of several separate capacities — its components
- gives an obvious sense of progress when it improves

👉 Programming toward a keystone means programming its COMPONENTS, not just attempting the keystone every week.

A member does not get a Muscle Up by doing failed muscle ups. They get it from false-grip hanging, pulling strength, and the dip transition — trained separately.

Three more words, about the shape of what you are writing:

### Program

One 6-week block. Identified by the date it starts.

### Class

A class TYPE, not a timeslot: Upper Body, Full Body, Lower Body, and optionally Handstand, Mobility, Flocomotion. Each type runs many times across the six weeks, so a class has no single day or time.

### Session

A timed block inside a class. Five per class: 10 / 5 / 15 / 15 / 15 minutes.

# 🔹 WHAT YOU NEED BEFORE YOU START

Gather these first. Steps 1 and 2 are impossible without the first three.

## Required

### 1. The last 3 programs

The written programs for the three most recent blocks, in the standard format.

This is what “what have we done recently” actually means. Without it you are guessing, and guessing always favours what you personally remember teaching.

### 2. The Movement Library

The full list of exercises, each with its discipline, line, importance and whether it is a keystone. This is the master sheet.

You need it to find alternatives, to see what a discipline actually contains, and to check you are using an exercise’s real name.

### 3. The Discipline & Keystone Frequency Guide

⚠️ THIS DOES NOT EXIST YET. It is the single biggest thing missing from this process.

It should list every discipline and every keystone, each with a target frequency — how often it should appear in a program. For example:

- Every program — e.g. Handstands, Push Up, the basic locomotions
- Every second program — e.g. Muscle Up - Rings, Front Lever
- Occasionally — e.g. Handstand - One Hand

Without it, Step 2 depends entirely on the programmer’s memory of what matters, which is exactly the judgement this playbook is meant to capture.

👉 Until it exists, use the Importance column in the Movement Library as a stand-in: 1 = every program, 2 = every second program, 3 = occasional.

### 4. The class structure and balance rules

The session timings and the rules a class has to satisfy. Both are in this playbook, further down.

### 5. Teacher feedback from the block just finishing

What worked, what did not, what the members responded to, what was confusing to teach.

Collect this BEFORE you start writing, not after.

## Helpful, but not required

### 6. The digital tools

The Movement Wheel, the Discipline view, and the Keystone breakdown. They make Steps 1, 2 and 4 much faster. Every step below can be done without them.

### 7. Equipment and space notes

Anything out of action, and anything new worth building a block around.

# 🔹 THE PROGRAMMING PROCESS

Eleven steps, in five phases. Steps 4 to 8 are a loop, not a straight line.

LOOK BACK (1–2) → DECIDE (3) → BUILD (4–7) → REFINE (8, loops back to 4) → SHIP & LEARN (9–11)

Set aside roughly half a day for steps 1 to 8 the first few times. It gets much faster with practice.

# PHASE A — LOOK BACK

## Step 1 – REVIEW THE LAST 3 PROGRAMS

Before deciding anything, find out what the members have actually been doing.

Read the last three programs and write down:

- Which disciplines appeared, and how often
- Which keystones were trained
- Any exercise that has appeared in all three — members are probably tired of it
- Anything that appeared once and then vanished — started and abandoned

👉 Output: a list of what HAS been covered recently.

On paper: three columns, one per program. Tick off each discipline as you see it. The gaps become obvious.

With the tools: the heat map does this automatically — hot means recently trained, cold means neglected.

⚠️ Do not skip to Step 3 because you already have an idea. The idea you arrive with is almost always a favourite, not a gap.

## Step 2 – IDENTIFY THE GAPS

Now compare what you found against what SHOULD be happening.

Go through the Discipline & Keystone Frequency Guide and mark each entry:

- 🟢 On track — trained about as often as intended
- 🟡 Getting cold — due, or slightly overdue
- 🔴 Neglected — well overdue, or never trained

Pay particular attention to:

- Disciplines that should appear every program but did not appear in the last one
- Keystones nobody has trained the components of for two or more blocks
- A whole pillar that has gone quiet — usually Object Play or Mobility

👉 Output: a shortlist of red and amber disciplines and keystones.

On paper: work down the frequency guide with three highlighters.

With the tools: the heat map colours this for you at discipline, line and exercise level.

# PHASE B — DECIDE

## Step 3 – CHOOSE THE GOALS

Pick what this block is FOR. Choose from the red and amber list, not from scratch.

A good block has:

- 2 to 3 discipline goals — areas getting real attention this block
- 1 to 2 keystone goals — landmark movements we are deliberately building toward
- Everything else at maintenance — present, but not the focus

⚠️ Resist choosing more. Six goals is the same as no goals: nothing gets enough time to improve, and the block feels like a grab bag.

Write the goals down in one sentence each, in plain language:

“This block we are building toward the Muscle Up, bringing back Object Play, and keeping handstands ticking over.”

👉 Output: a written goal statement. Everything after this is in service of it.

⚠️ Sanity check: would a member notice these goals by week 4? If not, they are too diffuse.

# PHASE C — BUILD

## Step 4 – SELECT EXERCISES

Now find the exercises that serve the goals.

For each KEYSTONE goal:

- Break it into its components — the separate capacities it is built from
- Pick exercises that train each component at a level your members can actually do
- Include the keystone itself only if members are close enough for it to be worth attempting

For each DISCIPLINE goal:

- Pick exercises spread across its different lines, not four from the same one
- Include an easy entry point and something to aim at, so every member has a version

Then add the NON-NEGOTIABLES:

- Anything marked “every program” in the frequency guide — handstands, the basic locomotions, pressing and pulling
- These are not goals. They are the baseline that runs underneath every block.

👉 Output: a pool of exercises, comfortably more than you need. Expect to cut some later.

On paper: work through the Movement Library discipline by discipline and list candidates.

With the tools: the Discipline view shows a whole discipline and its lines at once; the Keystone breakdown lists the components of a keystone and the exercises that train each; the Movement Wheel is good for spotting related things you would not have thought of.

## Step 5 – COMPILE THE CLASSES

Sort the pool into the class types.

- Upper Body — pulling, pressing, straight-arm, hanging
- Full Body — locomotion, object play, partner work, conditioning, anything integrated
- Lower Body — squats, hinges, single leg, hip and leg mobility, jumping

Plus any optional classes running this block: Handstand, Mobility, Flocomotion.

Rules of thumb:

- An exercise can appear in more than one class — that is normal and often desirable
- If a class has nothing serving a goal, the goals and the class types are out of step — go back to Step 4
- If one class is overflowing and another is thin, move things rather than padding

👉 Output: three (or more) buckets of exercises, one per class.

## Step 6 – BALANCE THE CLASS PACING

Now decide the SHAPE of each class, before you place a single exercise into it.

Each class is five sessions:

- Session 1 — 10 min — Warm Up or Game
- Session 2 — 5 min — Personal Goals (members choose; never programmed by the coach)
- Session 3 — 15 min
- Session 4 — 15 min
- Session 5 — 15 min

So you are programming FOUR sessions per class: 1, 3, 4 and 5.

Give each of sessions 3, 4 and 5 a role: Skill, Strength, Mobility, Conditioning, Prehab or Game.

Then check it against the Class Balance Rules below.

👉 Output: for each class, a named role and a rough intensity for each session.

## Step 7 – FILL THE SESSIONS

Place the exercises from Step 5 into the session shape from Step 6.

- Up to 4 exercises per session, running concurrently
- Put the exercise that most needs attention and coaching earliest, while people are fresh
- Keep the number of separate setups per session low — four different pieces of equipment in one 15-minute session is chaos
- Vary it across the six weeks where it makes sense, but do not change everything every week: members need repetition to improve

Write it up in the standard program format as you go, not afterwards.

👉 Output: a complete, written program.

With the tools: the Program Builder does this by dragging exercises into the session slots, and exports the program in the standard format.

# PHASE D — REFINE

## Step 8 – ITERATE AND FINE-TUNE

You now have a program that is technically correct. This step is what makes it good.

Read the whole thing back and check the fine-grained factors:

### Fun

Is there anything in here that members will be excited about? A block of nothing but strict strength work is correct and joyless.

### Social

Is there partner work, or a game, somewhere in the week? People come back for the room as much as the training.

### Muscle groups

Across the week, is anything getting hammered three classes running? Is anything untouched?

### Full body movement in every class

Even Upper Body day should have something whole-body in it — a locomotion, a carry, a game. Nobody should leave having trained only one region.

### Keystone component focus

Go back to your keystone goals. Are the components actually all covered? It is very easy to program two of the three and think you have covered it.

### Level spread

Is there a version for the newest member and something for the most advanced, in every class?

👉 Then LOOP BACK TO STEP 4 and swap exercises to fix whatever the check found.

⚠️ Expect to go round this loop two or three times. That is the step where a correct program becomes one people enjoy.

# PHASE E — SHIP & LEARN

## Step 9 – GET TEACHERS TO REVIEW

Send the written program to the coaches who will teach it, before it starts.

Ask specifically:

- Is there anything here you would not know how to teach or cue?
- Is there anything you cannot scale for a beginner or an injured member?
- Is there anything that will not physically work in the space, or clashes for equipment?
- Does it look fun to teach?

⚠️ “Looks good” is not a review. If nobody has a single question, they have not read it properly.

👉 Output: an amended program, and coaches who already know what is coming.

## Step 10 – RUN THE PROGRAM

Teach the block.

- File the final program where the previous ones live — it becomes the input to the next Step 1
- Note anything notable as it happens, rather than trying to recall it in week 6
- Modify for individuals as needed — see the Exercise Modification & Injury Playbook

## Step 11 – REVIEW AT WEEK 2

Two weeks in, check the program against reality. Do not wait until the end — there are still four weeks left to fix.

Ask the teachers:

- What is landing well?
- What is not working, and is it the exercise or the way it is set up?
- Is anything too hard or too easy for most of the room?
- Is anything taking much longer to set up than its session allows?

Then make small changes:

- Swap out an exercise that is not working — keep the session role the same
- Adjust a progression that is pitched wrong
- Do NOT restructure the block. Members need the remaining four weeks to be consistent.

👉 Output: a tweaked program, and notes for the next block’s Step 1.

# 🔹 CLASS BALANCE RULES

⚠️ DRAFT — these need Rod’s confirmation before they are treated as the standard.

Every class must satisfy all of these:

### Hard rules

- Session 1 is always a Warm Up or a Game
- Session 2 is always Personal Goals, and is never programmed by the coach
- Across sessions 3, 4 and 5: at least one must be Strength or Conditioning
- Across sessions 3, 4 and 5: at least one must be Skill or Mobility
- No more than 4 exercises running concurrently in any session

### Strong guidance

- Every class contains at least one full-body movement, whatever the class focus
- Every class contains at least one exercise serving a stated goal of the block
- Do not put your two most intense sessions back to back
- Every class has an entry point a brand-new member can do

👉 If a class fails a hard rule, it is not finished. Go back to Step 6.

# 🔹 FINAL CHECK — BEFORE YOU SEND IT OUT

### 🟢 GREEN — ready for teacher review

- Every goal from Step 3 is visible in at least two classes
- Every class passes all the hard rules
- Every keystone goal has all its components covered
- Every exercise name matches the Movement Library exactly
- The program is written up in the standard format

### 🟡 AMBER — fix before sending

- A goal appears in only one class
- A class is all strength, or all skill
- The same exercise appears in every single class
- A session needs four different equipment setups
- Nothing in the block is new to anybody

### 🔴 RED — do not run this

- No warm up in a class
- A whole class serves none of the goals
- A keystone goal with no component work at all — just the keystone itself
- Exercises nobody on the teaching team knows how to coach
- A repeat of the previous block

# 🔹 COMMON MISTAKES

### Programming from memory

“I feel like we have not done much pulling.” Check. Feelings about what has been trained are unreliable, and they favour whatever you taught most recently.

### Programming your own training

The block quietly becomes what the coach is personally working on. Watch for it.

### Too many goals

Six focuses means no focus. Members feel busy but do not improve at anything.

### Attempting keystones instead of building them

Six weeks of failed muscle up attempts builds frustration. Six weeks of false-grip hangs, pulls and dip transitions builds a muscle up.

### Changing everything every week

Variety is good; churn is not. Members need to repeat something to get better at it.

### Changing nothing for six weeks

The opposite failure. The same warm up for six weeks is a long six weeks.

### Writing it up afterwards

If the program is only in your head until week 1, the teachers cannot review it and the next block has nothing to look back at.

# 🔹 COACH EXPECTATIONS

A TMC coach writing a program is expected to:

- Look at the last three programs before deciding anything
- Program for gaps rather than preferences
- Choose a small number of clear goals and state them in writing
- Build keystones through their components
- Include the every-program non-negotiables regardless of the goals
- Check every class against the balance rules
- Write the program in the standard format, before the block starts
- Send it to the teaching team for review, and act on what comes back
- Review it at week 2 and adjust
- File it so the next programmer can look back at it

# 🔹 WORKED EXAMPLE — ONE BLOCK

### Step 1 – Review

Last three programs: lots of pressing and handstands. Object Play appeared once, in block 1. No pulling keystone trained since block 1. Bear Walk appeared in all three.

### Step 2 – Gaps

- 🔴 Object Play — one appearance in three blocks
- 🔴 Muscle Up - Rings — no component work in three blocks
- 🟡 Hip & Leg Mobility — due
- 🟢 Hand Balancing — well covered, keep ticking over

### Step 3 – Goals

“This block: build toward the Muscle Up, bring Object Play back properly, and keep handstands and splits ticking over.”

### Step 4 – Exercise selection

Muscle Up components → false-grip hanging, pulling to sternum, dip transition:

- Passive Hang, Active Hang (grip and shoulder endurance)
- Chin Up, Pull Up, Ring Row (pulling strength)
- Dip - Rings, Dip - Box (the dip transition)

Object Play, spread across its lines:

- 3 Ball Cascade (keystone, entry point)
- Bo-Staff - Palm Spin (a different line, and it looks impressive)

Non-negotiables: Handstand - Chest to Wall, Push Up, Bear Walk (swap for Monkey Crawl — Bear Walk has been in all three blocks).

### Step 5 – Classes

- Upper Body → the muscle up components
- Full Body → Object Play, locomotion, a game
- Lower Body → splits work, squats, jumping

### Step 6 – Pacing, Upper Body class

- Session 1 (10) — Warm Up: shoulder prep, hangs
- Session 2 (5) — Personal Goals
- Session 3 (15) — Skill: false grip hanging + ring support
- Session 4 (15) — Strength: Chin Up / Ring Row
- Session 5 (15) — Conditioning: Ring Dip + carries

Check: warm up ✓, strength present ✓, skill present ✓, four sessions programmed ✓.

### Step 8 – Fine tune

Read back: Upper Body has no full-body movement and nothing social. Swap the Session 5 carries for a partner-based conditioning game. Still trains the same thing, and the room is livelier.

# 🔹 DOING THIS WITHOUT THE DIGITAL TOOLS

Everything above works on paper. The tools save time; they do not make the decisions.

- Step 1 — lay the last three programs side by side and tick off disciplines as you find them
- Step 2 — work down the frequency guide with three highlighters
- Step 4 — read the Movement Library discipline by discipline and write candidates on cards
- Step 5–7 — sort the cards into class piles, then into sessions
- Step 8 — read the whole thing back against the fine-tuning list
- Steps 9–11 — unchanged; these are conversations, not tools

The one thing you genuinely cannot do without is the WRITTEN RECORD of the last three programs. If those are not written down, this process cannot start.

👉 That is the real reason we write every program down in the standard format.
